# Band-passed phase correlation earns a fallback role

Phase correlation (PC) is not worth trying as a replacement for KLT on this rig, but it is worth one cheap offline experiment and, if that passes, a narrow role as a recovery tracker inside Basalt. Our own numbers set the bar: on real checkerboard corners KLT has a median error of **0.11 px with 0% lost**, while OpenCV `phaseCorrelate` (coarse-to-fine, Hann window) has **0.35 px with 9% lost**. The literature suggests that much of that gap comes from two fixable choices: OpenCV's 5×5 weighted-centroid peak estimator, and full spectral whitening, which gives noise bins the same weight as signal bins. Our own DoG result already shows the second effect, because band-passing before PC cut losses under 7 px blur from **16.8% to 5.7%**. The best-value path has three steps. First, sweep peak estimators and spectral weightings offline on the data we already have. Second, only if PC then gets close to KLT and KLT is shown to lose tracks on the rig's dark, blurred turns, add band-passed PC as a re-acquisition and initialisation step inside Basalt's coarse-to-fine tracker. Third, try a band-pass or CLAHE prefilter at OKVIS2's single image hook. Whole-image spectral VO (Fourier–Mellin, eFMT, o-eFMT), phase-congruency detectors (RIFT, RIFT2, HOPC) and dense local-phase flow do not fit a forward-looking 6-DoF fisheye on a Pi 5 CPU. I found no public Basalt, OKVIS2, VINS-Fusion, OpenVINS or ORB-SLAM3 fork with a phase-based front end, so anything we build here has no reference implementation to copy. Every literature number below was measured on another sensor and dataset. Each is a candidate to test on the rig, not a prediction for it.

## Our 0.35 px PC result is likely part estimator bias and part noise weighting

**The peak estimator is the first suspect.** OpenCV's `phaseCorrelate` finds the integer peak with `minMaxLoc` and refines it with a 5×5 intensity-weighted centroid. It applies a window only when the caller passes one ([OpenCV phasecorr.cpp](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/modules/imgproc/src/phasecorr.cpp)). Hrazdíra, Druckmüller & Habbal benchmarked this exact estimator ("sPC, as implemented in OpenCV") on SDO/HMI white-light solar images with 1,000 sub-pixel shifts per size. Its mean absolute error stayed at **about 0.15–0.18 px at every size from 32×32 to 1024×1024**. Their iterative upsampled PC (IPC) reached **0.036 px at 32×32** and 0.011 px at 1024×1024 ([Hrazdíra et al. 2020, ApJS](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4365/ab63d7)). An error floor that does not shrink with more data points to systematic bias (pixel locking toward the integer grid) rather than noise.

Ren, Jiang & Vlachos tested on five 8-bit test images (four at 512×512, one at 200×200) with 289 synthetic shifts made by linear interpolation and a Blackman window. Two-sided 3-point estimators did best there: Ren's two-sided linear weighting gave **(0.037, 0.038) px** mean absolute error in (x, y) and a 3-point Gaussian fit gave (0.068, 0.064) px. Foroosh's closed form gave (0.115, 0.134) px, the quadratic fit (0.111, 0.113) px, and Stone's phase-plane fit (0.720, 0.692) px. Under additive noise, Gaussian and Ren stayed "the best and robust", while sinc and esinc fits were "very sensitive to noise" ([Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)). Their linear-interpolation shifts low-pass the moved image, which favours smooth-peak estimators. That test design is closer to our blurred frames than to sharp synthetic data, but it still is not our sensor.

**Full whitening is the second suspect, and our DoG result supports it.** Phase normalisation divides every frequency bin by its magnitude, so in a dark, gain-lifted or blurred patch the many noise-dominated high-frequency bins get equal votes. scikit-image's documentation says phase normalisation "is not very robust to noise" and that the unnormalised form "may be preferable" at high noise ([skimage docs](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/api/skimage.registration.html)). The Aoki/Ito group's band-limited POC weights the spectrum to suppress aliasing and noise. They report **0.05 px with 32×32 windows** using a coarse-to-fine pyramid, measured on their own test images, not on a low-light camera ([Miura et al., SICE 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf)). In audio time-delay estimation with reverberation and noise, partial whitening at **β≈0.4** beat both full PHAT and no whitening ([partial whitening paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224694462_Audio_signal_delay_estimation_using_partial_whitening)). I found no image-domain study that tunes this exponent. Gradient correlation (PC on complex gradient images gx + i·gy) is a fourth version of the same idea. Its authors report that it avoids "the low-pass nature of images, interpolation errors, border effects, and aliasing" on whole images ([Tzimiropoulos et al., TPAMI 2010](https://typeset.io/pdf/robust-fft-based-scale-invariant-image-registration-with-1f7c3ho3jv.pdf)). DoG prefiltering, band-limited POC, partial whitening and gradient correlation all down-weight low-SNR bins. Our measured drop in losses from 16.8% to 5.7% under 7 px blur is the only patch-level blur result I found for any of them.

**Small windows explain part of the 9% loss.** On 16–36 px solar Shack–Hartmann subimages, Löfdahl found that unnormalised FFT correlation with a Hamming window was competitive only for shifts under 1 px. Beyond that, "the number of outliers increases, the random error increases, and the shifts are systematically underestimated" because of DFT wrap-around. A spatial-domain squared-difference search with 2-D quadratic interpolation stayed below 0.02 px RMS at 16×16 without noise ([Löfdahl 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.3401)). Uncorrected rotation adds error too. By my geometric estimate (not a published measurement), a 2° inter-frame roll moves the corners of a 32 px patch by about 0.8 px, which smears a translation-only PC peak. KLT absorbs that through its SE2 or affine warp. Both problems point to the same remedy: use the MPU6500 to pre-shift and derotate each patch before PC, so the residual shift stays well under a pixel or two.

**The DoG result also explains why KLT barely benefits from band-passing.** With DoG (1–8 px) before KLT, the dark+blur median moved only from **0.16 to 0.15 px**. That fits the literature. In Park, Schöps & Pollefeys's dense RGB-D alignment study, subtracting an 11×11 local mean (the low-cut half of a DoG) lowered drift on their real Kinect sequences with illumination change from **5.56 to 4.90 cm/s**. Gradient magnitude (4.33) and census (4.23) did better, and census cost roughly 10× the runtime on a GTX 950 ([Park et al., ICRA 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/park2017icra.pdf)). In the Bit-Planes ablation on synthetic small translations, larger smoothing kernels raised error, because "large kernels wash out the detail needed for small motions" ([Alismail et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1604.00990)). My reading, which is untested, is that KLT's least-squares update already weights each frequency by its gradient energy and so behaves like a matched filter, whereas fully whitened PC does not. A prefilter therefore has little to correct in KLT and a lot to correct in PC.

## Six candidates, ranked by expected value per unit of effort

The ranking assumes the realistic job for a phase method on this rig is robustness where KLT fails, not precision. On sub-pixel accuracy, the rig data already favour KLT by a factor of three. One caution applies to every row: a lower per-patch error does not guarantee a lower trajectory error. The KLTNet preprint reports that putting RAFT-Sparse in place of KLT in VINS-Mono "did not consistently help" ([KLTNet, arXiv 2608.24544](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24544)). So any candidate that passes its patch-level test still has to win on full-pipeline ATE on our own recordings.

| Rank | Candidate | Where it plugs in | Effort | Expected value | First test on the rig |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | PC estimator and weighting sweep: 3-point Gaussian, Ren two-sided, IPC, matrix-multiply DFT; weighting α∈{1, ~0.4–0.7}, DoG, gradient correlation; Hann vs Blackman; IMU pre-shift and derotation | Offline harness only; no SLAM code touched | About 1–2 days | High information value; decides whether ranks 2 and 5 are worth building | Median px error and % lost on the existing checkerboard and dark+blur sets, against KLT's 0.11 px / 0% |
| 2 | Band-passed, IMU-seeded PC as a re-acquisition and initialisation step for Basalt tracks that fail the forward–backward check or start at the coarsest level; KLT does the final refinement | New `optical_flow_type` in Basalt's factory, reusing `trackPoint`'s coarse-to-fine loop | About 1–2 weeks | Medium, but only if rank 1 brings PC close to KLT and Basalt is shown to lose tracks on blurred turns | Track survival, ATE and Pi 5 ms/frame on rig recordings, with and without |
| 3 | Band-pass prefilter for Basalt's pyramid, per level and sign-aware | `processFrame`, before `setFromImage` | About 2–4 days | Low to medium: precision gain measured at 0.01 px; the effect on loss rate is unmeasured | KLT loss rate with and without on the dark+blur set |
| 4 | Band-pass or CLAHE prefilter for OKVIS2 | `ThreadedSlam::getNextFrame` | About 1 day, plus retuning `absolute_threshold` | Medium for OKVIS2's survival on the darkest frames; no published number for this prefilter | OKVIS2 tracking failures on the darkest rig recordings |
| 5 | PC sub-pixel refinement of BRISK matches, or a patch tracker ahead of `matchMotionStereo` | OKVIS2 `Frontend.cpp` | Weeks | Low or unclear | Only if rank 1 passes and OKVIS2 is the chosen system |
| 6 | Monogenic local-phase residual inside Basalt patches | New patch type | Research project | Low on current evidence | None planned |
| — | Whole-image FMT, eFMT or o-eFMT; sub-image FMT VO; RIFT/HOPC phase-congruency detectors; DPCN++ | — | — | Not worth pursuing | — |

Rank 1 comes first because it is cheap and its answer gates everything else. Hrazdíra's sPC floor and Ren's estimator table suggest a large share of our 0.35 px is estimator bias. If a 3-point Gaussian or IPC on band-passed, IMU-seeded patches still sits well above KLT's 0.11 px, PC has no precision role, and the only question left is robustness. Cost is not a reason to rule PC out. As a rough operation count (my textbook estimate, not a measurement), one 32×32 real-input FFT is about 26k flops. One PC per patch, with the reference spectrum cached, comes to about 60–100k flops, against about 200k for a three-level, 21×21, ten-iteration pyramidal KLT. I found no measured per-patch PC or KLT timing on any Raspberry Pi or ARM CPU, so a Pi 5 micro-benchmark belongs in the same harness. The non-phase control for ranks 2 and 3 should be a census or gradient-magnitude residual, because Park et al. found those the strongest answer to real illumination change on their RGB-D data ([Park et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/park2017icra.pdf)).

Rank 2 targets PC's real structural strength. It needs no iterations, has no convergence basin, and captures any shift that fits inside the window. Those properties matter exactly where KLT's Gauss–Newton step diverges, on large blurred inter-frame motions. Its value depends on a fact we have not yet measured: whether Basalt actually drops tracks on the rig's turns. KLT's 0% loss on checkerboard corners says nothing about that regime, and the dark+blur KLT test reported precision, not loss. Rank 3 is a weak candidate on current evidence, because the measured precision gain is a hundredth of a pixel, but it could matter if it lowers the loss rate. Rank 4 is cheap and needs no architectural change, and it addresses OKVIS2's known weak spot (next paragraph). Its expected gain comes from steadier Harris detection, not from better matching.

The dataset evidence on which system degrades how is mixed, and none of it uses a rolling-shutter fisheye with gain-lifted underexposure. On "Dark EuRoC" (EuRoC V2_01 darkened synthetically by a gamma curve at 12 levels), OKVIS v1 and ORB-SLAM3 showed tracking failures or large drift at the two darkest levels, while Basalt stayed stable. On OIVIO (stereo in mines and tunnels lit only by an onboard light), Basalt averaged **0.192 m** RMSE against ORB-SLAM3's 0.065 m. On UMA-VI (handheld, low texture, sudden lights-off), OKVIS beat Basalt on **7 of 10** sequences ([AirSLAM, arXiv 2408.03520](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03520)). The pattern is that optical-flow front ends degrade gradually while descriptor front ends fail abruptly at the darkest levels. That is why a prefilter is worth more for OKVIS2's detector, and a recovery tracker worth more for Basalt.

## Basalt has two clean hooks, and a mean-normalised uint16 residual sits between them

All of Basalt's optical flow lives in header-only templates under `include/basalt/optical_flow/`. The notes cite upstream commit `0f3b2b52` ([optical_flow dir](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/tree/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/include/basalt/optical_flow)). The default `frame_to_frame` tracker runs as follows. `processFrame` (L111) builds one `ManagedImagePyr<uint16_t>` per camera at L123–131 and L145–152. `trackPoint` (L243–270) runs coarse to fine and calls `trackPointAtLevel` (L273–305), which makes up to `optical_flow_max_iterations` inverse-compositional SE2 Gauss–Newton steps. A forward–backward check (L214–226) then rejects any track that does not return to within `optical_flow_max_recovered_dist2` of its start; the default is 0.09, and the shipped EuRoC config uses 0.04 ([frame_to_frame_optical_flow.h](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/include/basalt/optical_flow/frame_to_frame_optical_flow.h#L111-305), [vio_config.cpp](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/src/utils/vio_config.cpp#L48-55)). `OpticalFlowFactory` picks the tracker class from the `optical_flow_type` string ([optical_flow.cpp L44–110](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/src/optical_flow/optical_flow.cpp#L44-110)).

**The matcher hook for rank 2** is a new class registered in that factory, for example `"phase_frame_to_frame"`, copied from `FrameToFrameOpticalFlow`. Inside it, PC either replaces or seeds the per-level update in `trackPoint`. The forward–backward check, the epipolar filter (`optical_flow_epipolar_error`, default 0.005) and the `OpticalFlowResult` contract to the back end stay unchanged, so the estimator never sees the swap. The swap is self-contained in Basalt's code, but PC yields translation only. The SE2 rotation part therefore has to come from the gyro, or from one Gauss–Newton step after PC. Per the notes' clone, the Monado fork (`gitlab.freedesktop.org/mateosss/basalt`, commit `df6e970c`) adds two relevant features: `optical_flow_image_safe_radius`, which masks the black fisheye corners, and a landmark-recall feature ([mateosss vio_config.h](https://gitlab.freedesktop.org/mateosss/basalt/-/blob/df6e970c8da7636eb401a09e3317fbeaaf829b9a/include/basalt/utils/vio_config.h), [MR !35](https://gitlab.freedesktop.org/mateosss/basalt/-/merge_requests/35)). Both are useful for our fisheye whichever matcher wins.

**The prefilter hook for rank 3** is the image passed to `setFromImage`. Filter into a new buffer there; `new_img_vec` itself is forwarded downstream to the GUI and to recall, so it must stay unfiltered. The trap is the residual. Basalt's patch residual is a locally scaled SSD: each sample is divided by its patch mean (`residual[i] = num_valid_points * val / sum - data[i]`), and any patch with a mean near zero is marked invalid ([patch.h L165–207](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/include/basalt/optical_flow/patch.h#L165-207)). A DoG output is zero-mean and signed, but the pyramid is `uint16_t`; 8-bit input is shifted left by 8 bits when loaded ([dataset_io_euroc.h](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/include/basalt/io/dataset_io_euroc.h#L105-130)). A raw DoG would therefore invalidate most patches. There are two fixes. Adding a constant offset turns the residual into plain SSD on the band-passed image, which loses gain invariance; the DoG's low-cut partly compensates. Alternatively, write a signed or float patch type with a zero-mean residual, which means touching the templates. The binomial pyramid (kernel 1-4-6-4-1, [image_pyr.h](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt-headers/-/blob/b7f915b195c87c8373bf93f97a0710bcdaffd718/include/basalt/image/image_pyr.h#L71-141)) adds a second interaction. An 8 px low-cut applied at level 0 becomes 1 px at level 3 and strips the coarse content that large-motion capture depends on. Filtering each level with a fixed σ after the pyramid is built avoids that. FAST detection runs on an 8-bit copy of level 0 ([keypoints.cpp L161–235](https://gitlab.com/VladyslavUsenko/basalt/-/blob/0f3b2b52c807f70ff4e2973ce253c73329eea7bc/src/utils/keypoints.cpp#L161-235)), so a prefilter also changes detection unless a raw copy is kept for it. Neither upstream nor the Monado fork contains any CLAHE, histogram equalisation or other preprocessing.

## OKVIS2 has one image hook and no patch tracker to swap

OKVIS2 (commit `a2ea0068`) does no image preprocessing. `ThreadedSlam::getNextFrame` copies each camera image into a `cv::Mat` named `filtered`, which is never actually filtered, and passes it to `multiFrame->setImage` ([ThreadedSlam.cpp L251–268](https://github.com/smartroboticslab/okvis2/blob/a2ea00688cd10988aae7bd52ab7935ce9a657ec0/okvis_multisensor_processing/src/ThreadedSlam.cpp#L251-L268)). The authors wrote a CLAHE call but left it commented out (`//mClahe = cv::createCLAHE(3.0, cv::Size(8, 8));`). Detection is a BRISK scale-space Harris detector, and matching is brute-force Hamming distance on 48-byte BRISK descriptors with a threshold of 60. That matching runs in `matchToMap` (L1260), `matchMotionStereo` (L1920) and `matchStereo` (L2167), templated over the equidistant fisheye model among others ([Frontend.cpp](https://github.com/smartroboticslab/okvis2/blob/a2ea00688cd10988aae7bd52ab7935ce9a657ec0/okvis_frontend/src/Frontend.cpp#L2579-L2600)).

**The prefilter hook for rank 4** is that one line in `getNextFrame`. Detection, description and all three matchers read the same image, so one change covers everything. The output must go back to `CV_8U`. BRISK's descriptor compares pixel intensities, so it is already invariant to monotonic brightness changes, and a band-pass would mainly steady the Harris responses on gain-amplified noise. `FrontendParameters.absolute_threshold` (the Harris noise floor, [Parameters.hpp L125–135](https://github.com/smartroboticslab/okvis2/blob/a2ea00688cd10988aae7bd52ab7935ce9a657ec0/okvis_common/include/okvis/Parameters.hpp#L125-L135)) will need retuning after any prefilter. **A phase matcher (rank 5) is a larger change here than in Basalt.** OKVIS2 has no KLT stage to replace, so PC would have to be either a new temporal-association step before `matchMotionStereo` or a sub-pixel refinement of BRISK keypoints after matching. I found no numbers on how much BRISK keypoint localisation error contributes to OKVIS2's trajectory error, so the payoff of that refinement is unknown.

## Whole-image spectral VO and phase-congruency features break this rig's assumptions

**Fourier–Mellin VO estimates four degrees of freedom and assumes one dominant plane.** In the thesis that presents eFMT, its author writes that classic FMT "only works when the camera does not roll or pitch" and is "limited to single-depth environments". eFMT extends it to several depths but stays 4-DoF with a down-looking camera ([Xu PhD thesis, ShanghaiTech](https://robotics.shanghaitech.edu.cn/sites/default/files/files/Qingwen_Xu_PhD_Thesis_English.pdf)). Its successor o-eFMT reports an ATE mean of **15.7 m against 165.3 m for ORB-SLAM3**. That was measured on a DJI M300 RTK UAV with a down-looking camera, images cropped to 512×512, scale aligned after the fact, and DSO failing outright. o-eFMT took **0.143 s per registration** on an i7-7700 ([o-eFMT, arXiv 2307.10015](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.10015)). Those wins come from feature-poor aerial scenes at low frame rates. A forward fisheye on a 50 cm robot pitches and rolls, sees 0.3–5 m of depth with strong parallax, and turns yaw into horizontal image shift rather than in-plane rotation, so every core assumption fails. The only route the literature shows to 6-DoF uses FMT on many sub-images feeding a five-point RANSAC. Even then, on Xu's synthetic test with 5–10° inter-frame rotations on omni images, 32×32 sub-images gave **about 5–10° of rotation error**, and the thesis recommends windows of at least 64×64 px (Xu thesis, Table 2.1). The MPU6500 gyro already supplies the rotation that whole-image spectral methods spend most of their compute estimating. Gyro-derotated, per-patch PC (rank 2) is the part of this literature that transfers.

**Phase-congruency features solve a different problem at a cost the Pi cannot pay.** RIFT and RIFT2 match **94–100%** of multimodal remote-sensing pairs (SAR-optical, IR-optical, map-optical, day-night), where SIFT matches 2–60%. Their localisation RMSE of about **1.8–3.1 px** is measured under 3 px inlier thresholds, and RIFT2 needs 11–13 s per pair in C++ on an i7-8550U ([RIFT, arXiv 1804.09493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.09493.pdf); [RIFT2, arXiv 2303.00319](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.00319)). The RIFT authors themselves call the raw phase-congruency map "sensitive to noise". On 80×60 thermal images on an i7-3687U, phase-congruency detection took **5.2 ms against 90 µs for FAST** ([thermal stereo, arXiv 1912.00138](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.00138)). Between consecutive frames from one visible camera, the brightness change is roughly monotonic, and Basalt's mean-scaled residual and BRISK's comparisons already absorb that. The extra radiometric invariance of phase congruency buys nothing here, and its noise sensitivity works against us.

**Local-phase optical flow is accurate on clean synthetic data but not noise-immune.** Fleet & Jepson reached **0.23–0.32°** average angular error on the synthetic Translating Tree. On Yosemite it was no better than Lucas–Kanade (4.29° at 34% density, against LK's 4.10° at 35%), and it used 21 frames of temporal support ([Barron, Fleet & Beauchemin, IJCV 1994](https://www.csd.uoc.gr/~hy576/bibliography/PerformanceOpticalFlow.pdf)). A two-frame monogenic method held **13.5–15.8°** under ±50% brightness change on Yosemite, where 2-D CLG collapsed to 47–52°. On clean frames it scored 3.37°, but additive noise at 10 dB SNR raised that to **14.16°** ([Zang et al., SSVM 2007](https://www.mia.uni-saarland.de/Publications/zang-ssvm07.pdf)). That is an advantage over brightness-constancy flow with no photometric model, not over KLT with gain compensation. Every published real-time implementation I found ran on a GPU or FPGA ([Pauwels & Van Hulle, CVPRW 2008](https://mlanthology.org/cvprw/2008/pauwels2008cvprw-realtime)).

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question from "phase correlation or KLT?" to "where does KLT fail on our rig, and is band-passed, IMU-seeded PC better there?". Our own measurements already contain the key mechanism. Down-weighting low-SNR frequencies is what makes PC work on dark, blurred patches, and KLT gets that weighting for free, which is why DoG barely moved its error. Neither the literature nor our data yet show KLT losing tracks in the regime where PC's iteration-free, wide capture range would pay off. That missing measurement, KLT and Basalt loss rates on the rig's dark, blurred turns, together with the one-to-two-day estimator and weighting sweep, decides whether any phase-based code goes into a SLAM front end at all.

If both results favour PC, the result would be new as far as public code goes: I found no VIO fork with a phase-based patch tracker, and no peer-reviewed PC-versus-KLT comparison of track loss or ATE on EuRoC, TUM-VI or KITTI. A rig result would be the first data point rather than a replication. That cuts both ways. There is no implementation to borrow, and no published failure case to warn us off. The evaluation has to end in full-pipeline ATE and Pi 5 frame time on our own recordings, not in per-patch pixel error.
