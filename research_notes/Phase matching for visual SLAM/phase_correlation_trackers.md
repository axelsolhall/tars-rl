# Phase correlation as a local patch tracker for visual odometry, and its sub-pixel estimators

Scope note: every number below was measured by the cited authors on their own data (mostly whole images of 100–1024 px, synthetic shifts, MRI or solar images). None of them predicts results on the TARS rig (IMX219 fisheye, rolling shutter, Pi 5, dark/noisy/blurred frames). The dataset and conditions are given next to each number.

## 1. Sub-pixel PC estimators: what exists, how accurate, which are biased

### Takeaway
Two-sided 3-point estimators applied to the PC peak (Gaussian fit, Ren et al.'s two-sided linear weighting) were the most accurate and the least sensitive to noise in the one full benchmark I could read. On 512×512 test images the mean absolute error (MAE) was 0.04–0.07 px. Foroosh/Hoge/quadratic fits gave 0.11–0.15 px, and Stone's phase-plane fit failed (about 0.7 px) in that test. Upsampled matrix-multiply DFT (Guizar-Sicairos) reached about 0.005 px MAE on whole images with κ=100. The 5×5 weighted centroid used by OpenCV's `phaseCorrelate` showed a flat error floor of about 0.15–0.18 px mean, at every image size from 32×32 to 1024×1024, in a solar-image benchmark. An iterative refinement (IPC) cut that floor to about 0.036 px at 32×32.

### Cited Findings
**OpenCV `phaseCorrelate` (what it actually does)**
- The integer peak comes from `minMaxLoc`. The code then refines it with `weightedCentroid(C, peakLoc, Size(5, 5), response)`. The source comment reads: "get the phase shift with sub-pixel accuracy, 5x5 window seems about right here...". The centroid is the intensity-weighted mean of x and y over the 5×5 box, clamped at the borders. The response is the 5×5 sum divided by M·N. — [OpenCV phasecorr.cpp](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/modules/imgproc/src/phasecorr.cpp)
- `phaseCorrelate` applies a window only if the caller passes one, and `createHanningWindow` is a separate helper. — [OpenCV phasecorr.cpp](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/modules/imgproc/src/phasecorr.cpp)
- A 2016 OpenCV Q&A thread reported a missing square root on the DC term in `magSpectrums()`. A respondent said this could add "a small bias" to the sub-pixel centroid. I found no linked fix. — [answers.opencv.org](https://answers.opencv.org/question/111618/phasecorrcpp-function-magspectrums-dc-component-missed-the-square-root-operation)
- Hrazdíra, Druckmüller & Habbal (ApJS 247:8, 2020) benchmarked on SDO/HMI white-light solar images. Their metric is the sum of 1,000 absolute sub-pixel errors, so divide by 1000 for the mean error per pair. Results for CC (cross-correlation) / PC (integer phase correlation) / sPC (non-iterative PC with a 5×5 centroid, "as implemented in OpenCV") / IPC (their iterative PC):
  - 32×32: 1165 / 1022 / 168 / 36
  - 64×64: 256 / 270 / 175 / 36
  - 128×128: 253 / 252 / 167 / 18
  - 256×256: 250 / 248 / 154 / 12
  - 1024×1024: 238 / 238 / 165 / 11

  IPC upscales the PC neighbourhood (bilinear upscaling, since bicubic and Lanczos gave "no statistically significant accuracy improvement") and iterates. The authors say this "significantly decrease[s] the bias of resulting shifts being close to an integer" (pixel locking). They report IPC beat the others at every size tested, from 32×32 to 4096×4096. — [Hrazdíra et al. 2020, ApJS](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4365/ab63d7). The paper's author proposed IPC for OpenCV, and a maintainer replied "Sounds useful". I found no evidence it was merged. — [OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/iterative-phase-correlation-pr/24234)

**Ren, Jiang & Vlachos 2010 (IEEE TIP 19(5):1379–1384): the most complete head-to-head I could read**
- **Setup:** 5 images (Airfield, Barbara, Image043, Pentagon at 512×512, Paris at 200×200), 8-bit. There are 289 2-D sub-pixel shifts in [−1, 1], generated by *linear interpolation*. A Blackman window was applied for all methods except Hoge. — [Ren et al. 2010 (author PDF)](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)
- **Mean absolute error in px, as (x, y):**
  - Hoge (SVD subspace): (0.145, 0.133)
  - Foroosh (sinc/Dirichlet closed form): (0.115, 0.134)
  - Stone (phase-plane fit): (0.720, 0.692)
  - Gaussian 3-point fit: (0.068, 0.064)
  - Quadratic 3-point fit: (0.111, 0.113)
  - Sinc fit: (0.083, 0.086)
  - esinc fit: (0.076, 0.085)
  - Ren's two-sided linear weighting: (0.037, 0.038)

  Standard deviation of the error in px: Gaussian (0.042, 0.040), quadratic (0.071, 0.075), Foroosh (0.112, 0.127), esinc (0.131, 0.164), Ren (0.027, 0.024). — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)
- **Behaviour across the shift range:** "Foroosh's method and ESinc fitting... suffer large estimate errors when the absolute values of sub-pixel shifts are more than 0.6". Sinc and quadratic fits give consistent estimates, and sinc beats quadratic. Hoge's method showed "occasional incorrect estimates". — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)
- **Additive noise** (Gaussian variance 0.005–0.045 and uniform variance 0.05–0.25, on images normalised to [0, 1]): "curve fitting using sinc and esinc is very sensitive to noise". Foroosh was third worst. Gaussian fitting and Ren's method were "the best and robust over the entire set of test conditions". — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)
- **Real MRI pairs** (Hoge's grapefruit set, 10 pairs, MSE as (x, y)):
  - Hoge: (0.367, 0.191)
  - Foroosh: (0.337, 0.195)
  - Balci & Foroosh: (0.586, 0.084)
  - Gaussian: (0.355, 0.031)
  - Quadratic: (0.333, 0.061)
  - Sinc: (0.349, 0.130)
  - esinc: (0.385, 0.051)
  - Ren: (0.349, 0.040)

  — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)
- **Formulas used there:**
  - Quadratic: Δx = (C(1,0) − C(−1,0)) / (2(2C(0,0) − C(1,0) − C(−1,0))).
  - Gaussian: the same form applied to the logs, i.e. ln C(1,0) − ln C(−1,0) over 2(2 ln C(0,0) − ln C(1,0) − ln C(−1,0)).
  - Foroosh's one-sided estimate: Δx = C(1,0) / (C(1,0) ± C(0,0)).
  - Ren's argument: a one-sided estimate is biased by the interference term even at zero shift, while a two-sided difference D = C(1,0) − C(−1,0) cancels it when the interference is even.

  — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf)

**Other comparisons (abstracts only)**
- Ren, Zhao, Ren & Cheng 2016 (IJICC) compared 8 methods on Barbara and Pentagon with Gaussian noise. Methods using dual-side peaks did better, dual-side interpolation beat curve fitting on noisy samples, and "Gaussian-based dual-side interpolation seems the best". — [Ren et al. 2016, IJICC](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJICC-03-2016-0009/full/html)
- Alba et al. 2015 (CVIU 223) compared six methods in 1D and 2D. Local centre of mass, sinc fitting and minimising the POC gradient magnitude were best under mild noise and low transformation complexity. Quadratic fitting, frequency-domain linear (phase-plane) fitting and upsampling were less consistent. — [abstract](https://orbis.uaslp.mx/vivo/display/scopus56)
- Vera & Torres 2008 (EUSIPCO) studied varying aliasing. Gaussian-fit methods (1D or 2D) were the least sensitive to heavily aliased imagery. — [abstract](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/49/700236.htm)
- Stone, Orchard & Chang (1999) showed standard PC loses much sub-pixel performance under even moderate aliasing. Their remedy was to discard unreliable (aliased) frequency components, which is the basis of the Stone 2001 phase-plane method. — [abstract](https://search.isc.ac/Inventory/49/1827184.htm)
- Yan & Liu (BMVC 2008) restate the critique by Foroosh et al. that Stone's approach "is rather inaccurate" because it needs frequency masking. They improve phase-plane fitting with a phase-fringe filter and the QMDPE robust estimator, and add "compound PC" to split multiple motions near depth discontinuities. — [Yan & Liu 2008](https://bmva-archive.org.uk/bmvc/2008/papers/292.pdf)
- An SVD + unified RANSAC method (remote sensing) reports beating existing methods under aliasing and noise, with less pixel locking than Hoge's SVD method. — [catalogue record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c532t-e51e4e243cc45bd06519d90d37bd1d211bba5ed6263caa12290b89bc53bb52db3)
- Foroosh, Zerubia & Berthod 2002 (IEEE TIP 11(3):188–200) show that for downsampled shifts the PC power sits in several coherent adjacent peaks: the polyphase components of a filtered unit impulse. This gives a closed-form sub-pixel solution. The abstract gives no error numbers. — [abstract](https://typeset.io/pdf/extension-of-phase-correlation-to-subpixel-registration-38la3txfr3.pdf), [dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/tip/ForooshZB02.html)

**Upsampled / matrix-multiply DFT (Guizar-Sicairos, Thurman & Fienup, Opt. Lett. 33(2):156, 2008)**
- The paper presents three algorithms based on nonlinear optimisation and matrix-multiply DFTs. They reach accuracy comparable to FFT upsampling with much less time and memory. — [abstract summary](https://insight.jci.org/references/scholar/328/B54), [MATLAB code](https://se.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/18401-efficient-subpixel-image-registration-by-cross-correlation)
- **scikit-image `phase_cross_correlation`:**
  - It implements this method: an FFT peak, rounded onto the 1/κ grid, then a matrix-multiply DFT over a region of `ceil(upsample_factor * 1.5)` samples around it.
  - `normalization="phase"` (the default) divides the cross-power spectrum by its magnitude.
  - The docs say phase normalization "works well in registering images under different illumination, but is not very robust to noise" and that "In a high noise scenario, the unnormalized method may be preferable".
  - Guizar-Sicairos's own method is the unnormalised cross-correlation.

  — [skimage docs](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/api/skimage.registration.html), [skimage source v0.24](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-image/scikit-image/v0.24.0/skimage/registration/_phase_cross_correlation.py)
- Unger & Brinker (BIOIMAGING 2021) ran Python/NumPy on a 2.4 GHz laptop CPU with κ=100. Images were about 200–1200 px, with translations of 0.5–3% of the image side and multiplicative Gaussian noise.
  - Matrix-multiply DFT (Guizar implementation): MAE 0.005 ± 0.011 px, runtime 0.324 ± 0.562 s.
  - Their projection-based hybrid: same MAE, 0.079 s.
  - 1-D image projections alone: MAE 0.017 px, 0.019 s.
  - MAE did not depend on image size, and runtime grew roughly quadratically for the MM-DFT.

  — [Unger & Brinker 2021](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2021/102429/102429.pdf)

**Phase-Only Correlation (Aoki/Ito group, Tohoku)**
- Their POC uses three pieces: (i) fitting an analytical peak model for the sub-pixel position, (ii) windowing against DFT periodicity, and (iii) spectrum weighting (band-limiting) against aliasing and noise. They report "0.01-pixel accuracy when image size is about 100×100 pixels". With a coarse-to-fine pyramid and sub-pixel block matching, they report "0.05-pixel accuracy when using 32×32-pixel matching window". — [Miura et al., SICE 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf)
- Takita et al. 2003 (IEICE Trans. Fund. E86-A(8):1925) is the original high-accuracy POC paper. It covers peak-model fitting and the spectrum-weighting extension; the about-1/100-px claim appears in snippets only. — [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1570291227387969152?lang=en)

**Gradient correlation (Argyriou & Vlachos; Tzimiropoulos et al.)**
- Tzimiropoulos, Argyriou, Zafeiriou & Stathaki (TPAMI 32(10):1899, 2010) replace intensities with complex gray-level edge maps (gx + i·gy) and use *normalised gradient correlation*. They say this "circumvents problems related to the low-pass nature of images, interpolation errors, border effects, and aliasing" and maps outlier errors to a uniform distribution. On real images they recovered translation, arbitrary rotation and scale up to 6 via log-polar FFT correlation. — [abstract](https://typeset.io/pdf/robust-fft-based-scale-invariant-image-registration-with-1f7c3ho3jv.pdf), [Kingston record](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/en/publications/robust-fft-based-scale-invariant-image-registration-with-image-gr-2/)

### Inferences
- Ren et al.'s synthetic shifts were made with *linear interpolation*, which low-pass filters the moved image. That favours estimators that assume a smooth peak (Gaussian, quadratic) and penalises those that assume an exact Dirichlet/sinc or a clean phase plane (Foroosh, Stone). Stone's "failure" there may partly be a test-design artefact; Foroosh used decimated high-resolution images instead.
- The 5×5 weighted centroid has an error that stays near 0.15–0.18 px at every image size in Hrazdíra et al. That pattern points to a systematic estimator bias (pixel locking: a centroid over a symmetric sinc-like peak with side lobes pulls toward the integer grid), not to noise. If so, a better peak estimator could remove a large share of the 0.35 px median seen on the rig's checkerboard test. Candidates are a 3-point Gaussian fit, Ren's two-sided estimator, IPC, or MM-DFT upsampling. This is a hypothesis to test on the rig's data, not a measured result.
- The best whole-image numbers (0.005–0.04 px) come from 100–512 px images. At 32×32 the best published PC numbers are about 0.036 px (IPC, solar images) and about 0.05 px (Aoki POC with pyramid). Errors grow as the window shrinks.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Guizar-Sicairos 2008: the Rochester PDF returned 404. Its own error-versus-noise numbers are missing.
- I could not retrieve full-text numbers for Foroosh 2002, Stone 2001, Hoge 2003, Balci & Foroosh 2006, Argyriou & Vlachos (gradient correlation, 2003–2006), Alba 2015 or Ren 2016. Only Ren 2010 gives their errors, and only under its own test design.
- No source gives the error of OpenCV `phaseCorrelate` on small (16–32 px) patches of natural camera images. The Hrazdíra numbers are solar granulation images.
- I found no published GitHub issue that quantifies the 5×5-centroid bias directly. The bias evidence above is the sPC column of Hrazdíra et al.

## 2. VO / SLAM / tracking systems that use (patch) phase correlation instead of KLT

### Takeaway
Phase correlation shows up in VO mainly as *whole-image or large-block* registration: ground-facing rover cameras with Fourier–Mellin, sonar mosaicing, radar odometry and video stabilisation. It is rarely used as a sparse per-feature tracker. I found no peer-reviewed study that swaps KLT for patch-level PC in a standard VO/VIO pipeline and reports ATE or track loss against KLT or ORB on a public dataset. The closest are the Aoki group's POC correspondence matching and a JSME 2014 abstract using POC for feature tracking on rough terrain, without a KLT comparison.

### Cited Findings
- **Ground-facing rover cameras:**
  - Kazik & Göktoğan (IEEE ICM 2011) did "Visual odometry based on the Fourier–Mellin transform for a rover using a monocular ground-facing camera". I confirmed the title and venue via citations only; no numbers retrieved. — [imreg_fmt docs](https://sthoduka.github.io/imreg_fmt/docs/fourier-mellin-transform)
  - Birem, Kleihorst & El Ghouti (J. Real-Time Image Processing 2018, DOI 10.1007/s11554-017-0706-3) used a Fourier-transform VO with a ground-facing monocular camera. A secondary summary reports 1.33% distance error and 17.2° orientation error, and says motion blur was not addressed because the tests were low-speed. Unverified against the paper. — [Bohrium summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/visual-odometry-based-on-the-fourier-transform-using-a-monocular-ground-facing-camera/813115676767027200-2645)
- FMT-based VO assumes one dominant depth plane and can fail when the dominant depth changes. That limitation motivated an extended FMT for multi-depth scenes. — [Remote Sensing 13(5):1000](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/5/1000)
- Morbidi & Caron (2017) used phase correlation for a dense visual compass, i.e. rotation-only heading from whole images. — [Morbidi & Caron](https://typeset.io/pdf/phase-correlation-for-dense-visual-compass-from-y4tqo96b8j.pdf)
- **Rough-terrain VO (JSME Robomec 2014, Tsunakawa & Kuroda):** they used POC for stereo matching *and* feature-point tracking in VO, claiming "high accuracy of subpixel and strong in change of lightness". The abstract shows no comparison with KLT. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsmermd/2014/0/2014__2A2-Q05_1/_article/-char/en)
- **Underwater forward-looking sonar** (Hurtós, Ribas, Cufí, Petillot, Salvi; J. Field Robotics 32(1):123–151, 2015): Fourier-based (PC) registration beat a state-of-the-art region-based technique for consecutive and non-consecutive views and was more robust in featureless areas. An earlier evaluation on real sonar data concluded that Fourier-based registration "stands as the more robust option" versus feature-based and area-based methods. These are self-reported. — [HW research portal](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/fourier-based-registration-for-robust-forward-looking-sonar-mosai/), [evaluation paper](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/evaluation-of-registration-methods-on-two-dimensional-forward-loo/)
- **POC sparse/dense correspondence (Aoki/Ito):**
  - Coarse-to-fine POC block matching gives about 0.05 px with 32×32 windows.
  - A GPU implementation is about 20× faster than CPU and about 4× faster than a naive GPU version.
  - A real-time stereo face system measures about 10,000 3-D points at 15 fps.
  - For POC multi-view stereo, the authors report better accuracy and lower cost than the NCC-based method of Goesele et al. on public MVS datasets.

  — [Miura et al. 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf), [Sakai et al.](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/PF2-18.pdf)
- **Edge video stabilisation** (Çığla, CVPRW 2024): global motion is estimated by PC on 4–8 "informative" 128×128 sub-blocks of 1080p frames. It ran at up to 150 fps on a Jetson Nano GPU, against 81 fps for a prior method on the same device. Accuracy was "around quarter sub-pixel" relative to the perturbation range, and 256×256 blocks handled larger shifts in under 10 ms. — [Çığla 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024W/EVW/papers/Cigla_Efficient_Video_Stabilization_via_Partial_Block_Phase_Correlation_on_Edge_CVPRW_2024_paper.pdf)
- **KLT as the baseline in VIO:** VINS-Mono uses KLT in its front end. KLTNet (2026 preprint) reports 49% lower average ATE than classical KLT in VINS-Mono, and notes that dropping RAFT-Sparse in place of KLT did not consistently help. So "beats KLT" claims need full-pipeline ATE, not just per-patch error. — [KLTNet arXiv 2608.24544](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24544)
- A dense-mapping monocular SLAM paper tracks features by maximising a spatial correlation over overlapping patches and compares against SURF/ORB/KLT. It is spatial correlation, not PC, and the snippet gave no numbers. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310464524_Dense_mapping_for_monocular-SLAM)

### Inferences
- Where PC wins in the literature, the scene is low-texture, speckled or low-resolution (sonar, ground texture) and the motion is close to a global translation or similarity. There, feature detectors struggle and the whole frame or a large block can be one PC window. That is a different regime from 16–32 px sparse feature patches on a fisheye camera with depth variation.
- None of these sources measures track loss under motion blur for PC versus KLT. The rig's DoG-prefilter result (16.8% → 5.7% loss under 7 px blur) has no published counterpart I could find.

### Gaps
- I found no endoscopy or planetary-rover system using patch-level PC with numbers. The MER/Mars rover VO used Harris corners plus correlation search, which I did not verify in this session.
- I found no peer-reviewed PC-versus-KLT comparison of tracking accuracy or lost tracks on EuRoC, TUM-VI or KITTI.
- I could not access the full texts of Kazik 2011, Birem 2018 or Tsunakawa 2014.

## 3. Windowing, patch size and spectral weighting for small (16–32 px) patches

### Takeaway
For small FFT windows, the dominant problems are wrap-around (periodicity) and the shrinking overlap as the shift grows. In a careful 16–24 px study (FFT cross-correlation, solar wavefront sensing), these caused outliers, more random error and a systematic *under-estimate* of larger shifts. A spatial-domain squared-difference search with 2-D quadratic interpolation beat the FFT method. Spectral weighting (band-limiting) and partial whitening are standard remedies, but I found no study that tunes the magnitude exponent α for image patches. The evidence for α≈0.4 comes from audio time-delay estimation.

### Cited Findings
- **Löfdahl (A&A 2010), solar Shack–Hartmann subimages of 16×16, 24×24 and 36×36 px:**
  - FFT covariance (CFF) with a Hamming window was compared against image-domain SDF/ADF² with 2-D quadratic interpolation (2QI).
  - SDF and ADF² with 2QI reached an RMS error below 0.02 px without noise and below 0.03 px with 0.5% noise, at 16×16. Going from 16 to 24 px cut the error by about 30%.
  - CFF was competitive only for shifts under 1 px, and only when given 24×24 against 16×16 for the others.
  - For larger shifts, "the number of outliers increases, the random error increases, and the shifts are systematically underestimated", which the author attributes to DFT periodicity.

  — [Löfdahl 2010, arXiv:1009.3401](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.3401). Note that CFF is unnormalised FFT cross-correlation, not phase-normalised PC.
- **Window shape (Löfdahl):** Hamming against a window with a flat central ~50% gave "mixed" results. The flat-top had larger errors but fewer outliers and less systematic underestimation, and "would have to be evaluated specifically for any new situation". A 1% intensity-bias mismatch wrecked the difference-based methods, so the mean should be subtracted first. — [Löfdahl 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.3401)
- **2-D versus 1-D peak interpolation (Löfdahl):** "the 2D IAs are clearly better than the 1D ones". Fitting polynomials of degree above 2 systematically underestimates small displacements, and degree-1 fits overestimate (citing November & Simon 1988). — [Löfdahl 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.3401)
- **Peak height and overlap:** as motion increases, the PC peak falls because the overlap area shrinks, while noise in the correlation surface rises. — [US patent 8842735](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8842735)
- **Spectral weighting in POC:** the Aoki group uses a spectrum-weighting / band-limited POC to suppress aliasing and noise in the high frequencies. They report BLPOC discriminates better than plain POC. — [Miura et al. 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf)
- **Partial whitening** (GCC-PHAT-β, audio delay estimation): partial normalisation beat both no whitening and full PHAT, and β≈0.4 was robust with both reverberation and independent noise. — [Audio signal delay estimation using partial whitening](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224694462_Audio_signal_delay_estimation_using_partial_whitening). A patent describes the same idea as a whitening strength α in [0, 1] on the magnitude denominator: 1 is full PC, 0 is plain correlation. — [US patent 11508086](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11508086)
- **Full normalisation and noise:** scikit-image's documentation says phase normalisation is "not very robust to noise" and that the unnormalised form may be preferable at high noise. — [skimage docs](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/api/skimage.registration.html). A Wikipedia talk page notes that for narrow-band or blurred images most frequencies hold noise, so their phases are random. This is a weak source and serves only as a consistency check. — [Talk:Phase correlation](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Phase_correlation)
- **Gradient correlation:** using complex gradient images instead of intensities is reported to avoid the low-pass nature of images, border effects and aliasing. — [Tzimiropoulos et al. 2010](https://typeset.io/pdf/robust-fft-based-scale-invariant-image-registration-with-1f7c3ho3jv.pdf)
- **Window choices in benchmarks:** Ren et al. 2010 used a Blackman window on every method except Hoge. — [Ren et al. 2010](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/29259/1/subpixel_r4.pdf). Yan & Liu name aliasing error and edge effects as the reasons for filtering and windowing. — [Yan & Liu 2008](https://bmva-archive.org.uk/bmvc/2008/papers/292.pdf)

### Inferences
- Full whitening (α=1) gives equal weight to every frequency bin. In dark, noisy or blurred patches most high-frequency bins are noise, which matches the losses the rig saw. Band-limiting (BLPOC), a DoG prefilter, gradient correlation and α<1 are four forms of the same fix: down-weight bins with low SNR. The DoG-prefilter gain the rig measured is consistent with this.
- A Hann window on a 32 px patch leaves an effective support of roughly half the patch. Pairing the window with patch size (e.g. 32 px for a 16 px effective support) and seeding PC with an IMU-predicted shift should cut wrap-around and overlap loss. Löfdahl's underestimate-at-larger-shifts result argues for keeping the residual shift small, for example via an IMU-predicted pre-shift or a pyramid. This is an inference to test.

### Gaps
- I found no image-domain study of PC accuracy versus magnitude exponent α, window type (Hann/Blackman/Tukey) and patch size at 16–32 px with real camera noise.
- I found no study of PC under motion blur at patch level.

## 4. Small rotations and scale changes: PC versus KLT's affine variants

### Takeaway
Plain PC models pure translation. The literature handles rotation and scale through Fourier–Mellin / log-polar resampling of the magnitude spectrum, which needs fairly large windows: the Aoki group reports 0.05° rotation accuracy at 128×128, and Tzimiropoulos et al. recover scale up to 6 on whole images. I found no paper that measures how PC's translation error or peak height degrades with small uncorrected rotation or scale on 16–32 px patches. KLT handles these directly through an affine or similarity warp in its Gauss–Newton update.

### Cited Findings
- **POC rotation estimation:** polar mapping of the amplitude spectrum turns rotation into translation, which POC then estimates. Accuracy is about 0.05° for 128×128 images. — [Miura et al. 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf)
- **Log-polar Fourier gradient correlation:** translation, arbitrary rotation and scale factors up to 6 on real images, a range the authors say no other Fourier-based correlation technique handled. — [Tzimiropoulos et al. 2010](https://typeset.io/pdf/robust-fft-based-scale-invariant-image-registration-with-1f7c3ho3jv.pdf)
- **Reddy–Chatterji style FMT:** rotation and scale become translations in log-polar Fourier magnitude. FMT VO assumes a single dominant depth plane. — [Remote Sensing 13(5):1000](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/5/1000)
- Yan & Liu's compound PC splits multiple translational motions inside one window, for example at depth discontinuities. That is a failure mode of single-peak PC on patches that straddle depth edges. — [Yan & Liu 2008](https://bmva-archive.org.uk/bmvc/2008/papers/292.pdf)

### Inferences
- A rotation θ (in radians) moves a point at radius r from the patch centre by about rθ. For a 32 px patch (corner radius about 22 px), 2° ≈ 0.035 rad gives about 0.8 px at the corners and about 0.35 px at 10 px radius. That smears the PC peak and shifts its centroid. This is my geometric estimate, not a published measurement. A Hann window weights the centre more heavily, which reduces the effect.
- On the rig, the IMU gives inter-frame rotation. Derotating each patch by the IMU-predicted rotation before PC is the PC counterpart of KLT's affine warp, and it avoids log-polar resampling of tiny patches. This is an untested design option.

### Gaps
- I found no paper with numbers on PC translation error versus uncorrected rotation or scale for small patches.
- I found no direct PC versus affine-KLT comparison under rotation or scale.

## 5. Computational cost: FFT sizes, operations per patch, embedded numbers

### Takeaway
Per-patch PC costs one forward FFT of the new patch (the reference FFT can be cached), one complex normalisation per bin, one inverse FFT and a peak fit. For 32×32 that is on the order of 10⁵ flops, roughly the same order as a pyramidal KLT on a 21×21 window. I found no independent benchmark of patch-level PC against KLT on a Raspberry Pi or Jetson CPU. The only embedded PC timings are block-level GPU stabilisation on a Jetson Nano. Upsampled-DFT refinement is the expensive estimator, while 3-point fits are close to free.

### Cited Findings
- **Jetson Nano GPU block PC (128×128 sub-blocks, 1080p):** 259.7 fps with 1 block, 147.8 fps with 4 blocks and 141.2 fps with 8 blocks, against 81.4 fps for a prior method on the same device and 60 fps on a TX1. That is up to about 7 ms per frame with 4–8 blocks. With 256×256 blocks, throughput fell to 165, 112 and 86 fps. — [Çığla CVPRW 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024W/EVW/papers/Cigla_Efficient_Video_Stabilization_via_Partial_Block_Phase_Correlation_on_Edge_CVPRW_2024_paper.pdf)
- **POC on GPU** is about 20× faster than CPU, enabling about 10k stereo correspondences at 15 fps. The hardware is not given in the overview. — [Miura et al. 2012](https://www.aoki.ecei.tohoku.ac.jp/~ito/TuA11-04.pdf)
- **Upsampled MM-DFT** (κ=100) on a laptop CPU in Python: 0.32 s mean per whole-image pair, growing about quadratically with image size up to 1.2 s. A projection-based pre-step cut this by about 75% at equal MAE. — [Unger & Brinker 2021](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2021/102429/102429.pdf)
- IPC is "slightly slower, due to its iterative nature, but not N times slower than standard phase correlation" because "the DFTs are NOT computed more times" (author's claim, no numbers). — [OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/iterative-phase-correlation-pr/24234)
- A vendor correlation tracker library, algorithm unnamed, reports 4.5 ms (128×128 window) and 11.5 ms (256×256) on one Raspberry Pi 4 core at 1.5 GHz, and 5.0 ms / 14.5 ms on one Jetson Nano core. These are vendor self-reports and unverified. — [CvTracker page](https://celery-guava-bjck.squarespace.com/correlation-video-tracker-lib)
- Löfdahl notes that ADF is fast because it maps well to SIMD instructions, especially for 8-bit data. ADF² adds negligible cost because only the ≤9 grid points used for interpolation are squared. — [Löfdahl 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.3401)

### Inferences
- **Rough operation count** (textbook radix-2 estimate of about 5·N·log2(N) real flops for a length-N complex FFT; my estimate, not measured):
  - A 32×32 complex 2-D FFT is about 5·1024·10 ≈ 51k flops, or about 26k for a real-input FFT.
  - Per patch: one forward FFT (reference cached) plus one inverse FFT plus 1024 normalisations (≈ a sqrt and a divide each) comes to about 60–100k flops.
  - Coarse-to-fine over 2–3 pyramid levels doubles or triples that.
  - Pyramidal KLT with a 21×21 window, about 10 iterations and 3 levels, at about 15 flops per pixel per iteration (bilinear sample plus accumulate) is about 200k flops.
  - Both are well under 1 ms per patch on one Cortex-A76 core in principle. Memory traffic and FFT-library efficiency at these tiny sizes will dominate, so benchmark on the Pi 5 rather than relying on these numbers.
- PC cost does not depend on the number of iterations and is deterministic. KLT's cost depends on convergence. A real-input FFT and caching the reference patch's spectrum, which KLT does not need, are the main levers for PC. Using 32×32 instead of 64×64 cuts cost about 4.5×.

### Gaps
- I found no measured per-patch PC timings on a Raspberry Pi 4/5 or on a Jetson CPU.
- I found no PC-versus-KLT timing study on ARM.
- OpenCV's `phaseCorrelate` pads inputs and allocates per call, and its per-call overhead on 32×32 patches is unknown. The rig's own profiling is the only reliable source here.
